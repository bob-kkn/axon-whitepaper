An economy is arriving where AI agents pay each other hundreds of times a second, in amounts smaller than a cent. Today's payment rails cannot settle a single one of those transactions. Axon's answer is not to improve the rails but to redefine the currency.

§ 01 WP 1.1–1.3

What is actually happening

An AI agent is no longer a tool that runs one human command at a time. Give it a goal and a budget, and it breaks the goal into pieces — and for the pieces it cannot do itself, it buys the services of other agents. A translation agent commissions review; a code-writing agent orders tests.

The human decides what is to be achieved; the agent decides what to buy, from whom, at what price. The transactions that result are unlike human commerce in three ways.

Far more frequent
Agents run around the clock and need milliseconds to decide on a purchase. While one top-level goal executes, hundreds of purchases cascade.
Far smaller
Finer division of labour improves both performance and cost, so summarising one document or verifying one function each becomes an independent paid transaction. Sub-cent amounts are the basic unit.
No human in the loop
Quotes, comparison, ordering and delivery confirmation are exchanged as protocol messages machines interpret, not contracts people read. There is nobody in the middle to press approve.

There is one more signal. Software billing has been decomposing for years — monthly seat → usage-based → per LLM token. The yardstick for value was already drifting toward compute.

§ 02 WP 2.1–2.5

Why today's rails cannot carry it

Card networks and bank transfers were designed around human transaction habits: a handful of payments a day, each worth at least a few dollars, between identity-verified people. Agent transactions break all three premises. This is not a gap that performance tuning narrows; it is a mismatch in design premises.

Fees
Card fees carry a fixed per-transaction component. As the amount shrinks, the fee rate diverges. On a sub-cent transaction the fee is tens to hundreds of times the value — the transaction simply does not hold up.
Settlement delay
A card payment looks instant, but the money actually moves one to two business days later. To a counterparty working in milliseconds, "next-business-day settlement" is not infrastructure but an obstacle.
Account model
Opening an account presupposes identity verification. An agent has no legal personhood, so it cannot open an account in its own name. Share one owner's card among hundreds of agents and per-agent limits, attribution and audit trails all become impossible.

Public blockchains are not the answer either

They remove the identity barrier, but the rest remains. Fees are charged independently of transaction value and spike under congestion, so the card network's problem reappears; finality takes seconds to minutes. Bundling transactions into blocks and having the whole network agree on their order is a choice made for security — but the delay and fees it costs are a fixed cost micropayments cannot bear.

§ 03 WP 3.1–3.4

Why compute, of all things, is money

The marginal cost of everything an agent does is dominated by inference compute. Both the party quoting and the party paying converge on the same question: how much compute does this task take. If that is so, it is natural for the currency of that economy to become compute itself.

This is a testable proposition, not a slogan. For anything to be money it must perform three functions.

Unit of account
Compare unlike goods on one scale. Pricing in compute puts price and cost on the same unit; pricing in fiat introduces two layers of noise — model repricing and exchange rates.
Medium of exchange
A translation agent needs review, but the reverse is not guaranteed. Demand for compute alone is universal — every agent runs on it, so a claim on compute is accepted by anyone.
Store of value
What you were paid today must hold its purchasing power tomorrow. A claim on compute is anchored to the work compute can perform, and that demand persists as long as the agent economy grows.

From the gold standard to the compute standard

Under the gold standard, trust in money came from convertibility: paper was a claim on a metal whose mining cost was real. Credit money replaced that anchor with an institution — a central bank manages value, and in exchange for the flexibility, money's value depends on trust in the issuer.

The compute standard returns the anchor to a physical good, but makes that good the factor of production of the economy in question. Gold's industrial utility was largely decoupled from monetary demand; compute is the raw material of the agent economy itself. Demand for the anchor is demand for economic activity — a more direct connection than the gold standard ever had.

"Compute keeps getting cheaper. Can a currency anchored to something whose production cost is falling hold its value?"

The strongest objection, and the whitepaper takes it head on. The answer splits in two. First, the layer of the unit. ACU is defined not by compute consumed as input (FLOPs) but by output — inference that meets a reference benchmark's quality. Progress that produces the same-quality output more cheaply does not dilute the unit; it lowers the cost of supply. Second, the layer of the money supply. New issuance is tied only to verified compute supply, and a portion of transaction fees is burned to offset supply pressure from the other direction.

§ 04 WP 4.1–4.4

Build the ruler first — the ACU

ACU (Agentic Compute Unit) is a unit, not a currency — the same way the metre measures length without being money. The currency is AXN; ACU is the ruler that fixes what that money refers to.

Why not measure in FLOPs? Because FLOPs are input, not output. The same FLOPs yield different quality depending on model and technique, and progress steadily reduces the FLOPs needed for output of a given quality. Anchor money to that ruler and its value thins out year after year.

So the ACU measures quality-adjusted compute against output: 1,000 tokens of inference output meeting reference benchmark quality. The usefulness of that output survives changes in production technology — being able to make it more cheaply does not change what 1 ACU means. The stability of the unit and the efficiency of production are decoupled. That is the structural answer to falling compute costs.

Models differ, so the network sets a conversion rate for each — how many ACU 1,000 tokens of a given model's output is worth — and periodically rebases it. The whitepaper names this rate-setting mechanism as the softest point in the design, and §10 returns to it.

§ 05 WP 5.1–5.4

When new money is created

Not by policy at a central bank, and not by winning a mining race. In Axon there is exactly one condition: compute was actually supplied, and that supply was verified.

01

A supply node performs compute and reports it to the network.

02

Verifiers re-execute a random sample to check the claim is true.

03

1 AXN is issued per verified 1 ACU.

04

0.97 goes to the supplier, 0.03 pays for verification.

Issuance is one direction; there is another. Every transaction carries a protocol fee of 0.1–0.5% of value, and part of it is burned — offsetting the supply pressure of a demand-growth phase from the opposite side.

The 3% verification tax is not a levy collected separately; it is a slice of the money being newly issued. The thing being verified pays for its own verification, so the verification layer sustains itself without an external subsidy.

There is no fixed total supply. Instead, because issuance is bound to compute supply alone, money cannot grow without supply and existing holdings cannot be diluted without issuance. The money supply grows at exactly the rate real compute supply grows.

§ 06 WP 6.1–6.5

The ledger splits into three layers

Tens of thousands of transactions a second, each worth less than a cent. No single ledger absorbs that. So the roles divide.

L1 — Settlement

Finalises balances and handles issuance, burn and disputes. A DAG with no blocks — instead of queueing transactions into blocks, each transaction directly verifies earlier ones and attaches in parallel. With no block to wait for, it targets sub-second finality.

L2 — Channels

Where the great majority of micropayments happen. Individual payments are never written to the ledger — they complete on the spot through an exchange of both parties' signatures — and only net amounts periodically settle to L1. No per-transaction recording cost means no fixed fee.

Identity

Gives each agent its own identity (DID) and binds that identity to the legal liability of its owner — a person or a legal entity. Not a human identity on loan: the machine gets its own name, and the owner carries the liability.

By analogy. L2 is a water meter: you do not call the bank for every drop — the meter simply turns. L1 is the monthly statement: the accumulated reading is settled once and made final. Millions of transactions leave one line in the ledger.

§ 07 WP 7.1–7.5

Make lying cost more than it pays

What if the claim "I supplied compute" is false? That is not ordinary fraud — it is counterfeit money. Getting currency issued for work never performed dilutes the holdings not of one counterparty but of every AXN holder.

So verification splits in two. Ordinary transactions, lightly: the buyer consuming the result is the natural verifier, and a wrong judgement harms only the parties to that payment. The issuance path, heavily: objective, adversarial verification that assumes a counterparty who intends to cheat.

Don't inspect everything. Just make fraud unprofitable.

Re-running every computation is unaffordable. The goal instead is to make the expected value of fraud negative — if what you lose when caught exceeds what you gain when not, a rational participant does not cheat.

Market

The real good being paid for is AI inference. That market is projected to grow from USD 106.15 billion in 2025 to USD 254.98 billion in 2030, a 19.2% CAGR — and inference spending is, by its nature, entirely recurring. Whatever share of it shifts into the ultra-small, high-frequency segment existing rails cannot handle becomes Axon's potential payment volume.

§ 09 Validation summary

We did not leave it as assertion

The whitepaper's claims were not left as sentences. Ten independent prototypes put them into code and stress-tested them under adversarial conditions. All three axes — issuance economics, verification feasibility, payment and verification plumbing — passed a first-pass validation.

15 / 60

scenarios whose verdict depends on the arrangement

The last figure is the one that matters. We shook the arrangement of transactions (actor identity, transaction nonce) across twenty arrangements on each axis, and found fifteen scenarios whose verdict rested on the arrangement rather than the rules. We did not fix them. We published the number.

This repository runs on one rule: publish only what was measured; write nothing that was not. Corrections are appended as history rather than erased. The validation summary records what broke with the same weight as what held.

What the prototype does not establish is equally clear: whether "compute is money" is a sound thesis, whether any economic parameter is right, and how a real network behaves under latency, partitions and rational adversaries.

§ 10 WP 11.1–11.3

What we have not answered

The whitepaper's rule is this: what has a mitigation is set down with its mitigation; what lacks a sufficient one is set down as insufficient. Below is the latter.

!

Consensus bootstrap. A purpose-built ledger does not inherit the accumulated security of an existing chain. How it withstands attack while the network is small is the single largest unanswered question in the paper.

!

The conversion-rate oracle. If a model's rate is inflated, its suppliers gain real value without committing fraud in the sampling sense. The economic countermeasure is unsettled.

!

Misjudgement is confiscation. Inference is non-deterministic, so an honest supplier slashed in error has property destroyed. This is recorded as a risk in its own right.

!

Securities characterisation. The criteria vary by jurisdiction, and a token designed for utility can still be characterised otherwise. It is the only item on this list that cannot be corrected after the fact.

!

Early liquidity and volatility. The argument that arbitrage between issuance and burn converges the price holds only when both directions are open and liquid. Before the flywheel turns, neither is.

What is deliberately left open

Unsettled and unsettleable are not the same thing. The following are open by intent at Phase 0.

Genesis quantity
Announced by governance before mainnet. The ratios are already public — ecosystem reward pool 55% / operator and team 17.5% / investors 17.5% / foundation reserve 10%.
Fee allocation
How the fee splits between burn and treasury is a governance parameter.
Phase thresholds
Each phase's numeric thresholds are published at the close of the preceding phase.
Team composition
Not carried in this edition. The ask at Phase 0 is design review, and the party receiving the operator and team allocation is disclosed when that allocation is actually fixed.
§ 11 WP 10.3

Advanced by criteria, not by dates

The roadmap carries no calendar dates. A date, if missed, becomes a cost in trust; if conditions are sacrificed to keep it, a larger cost. Each phase advances only when it has met its stated exit criteria.

PHASE 0

Whitepaper and community — current. Publication, design review, early partners. Exit: design-review feedback incorporated; testnet scope and verification plan fixed

PHASE 1

Testnet — running sampling verification and the L2 channel for real, re-validating the draft parameters against measurement. Exit: parameters fixed; target detection rate and finalisation time reproduced; external node participation held above a pre-announced threshold

PHASE 2

Mainnet — genesis allocation, compute-standard issuance begins, initial demand ignited. Exit: the ignition workloads' share of total payment volume falls below the announced threshold and stays there — the flywheel turns on its own

PHASE 3

Expansion — TEE preference path, zkML research, jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction regulatory work. An ongoing phase, managed by item-specific criteria

Phase 2's exit criterion is worth a second look. The workloads the operator and its partners inject at mainnet launch are an ignition device, not a permanent one. The phase graduates only when their share has fallen far enough and stayed there. Which is to say: a network whose ignition never switches off has no right to speak of expansion.

What we are asking for

Not endorsement. The strongest objection you can make.

Axon is at Phase 0 — whitepaper publication and design review. Nothing is deployed and the economic parameters are draft values. At this stage we invite participation in three ways: feedback on the design review, participation as supply and verification nodes on the Phase 1 testnet, and early workload partnerships.
